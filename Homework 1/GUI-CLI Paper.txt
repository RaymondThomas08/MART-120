The Difference Between the GUI and the CLI 
Despite how vast and interconnected a computer is, there are two main ways to navigate around it, through pictures and through words. One option operates on visuals while the other operates on clear, response based text. Though they both have different methods of providing information, and different levels of intuitiveness, they each execute the purpose of accessing the directories within, and what lies within said directories of, your computer environment.
The graphical user interface is a visual version of your computer environment. Abbreviated as GUI, it is represented by interactive icons such as folders, windows, and files, and allows users to access directories and interact with their computer through these icons. It is simple, meant to facilitate organization and make understanding it simpler through its comparisons to an actual desk, and is very user-friendly. 
The command-line interface, abbreviated as CLI, is a text-based version of your computer environment. It works similarly, but instead of having pictures to represent actions, it has users type out commands into a terminal in order to access directories and interact with their computer. Once a command is entered, the computer responds back to it, and to enter the next, the user responds back to the computer. It is a bit more complicated to operate, requiring a strong knowledge of commands as well as when and where to use them, but is direct, efficient, and accurate.
Both of these interfaces are options for interacting with a computer’s operating system, but they come with key differences. Though easier for newer users to manipulate, the graphical user interface works much slower to execute commands and is less precise in executing commands than the command-line interface. It does not provide you with a list of your previous actions like the CLI does, making it harder to retrace and repeat your steps within the interface. On the contrary, the command-line interface is much harder to access and hone, being more suitable for experienced users. In the CLI it is easier to get certain actions wrong, and especially to mistype them if you don’t understand what they are, carrying a steep learning curve for beginners, presenting as less intuitive than the GUI, and requiring the memorization of many commands. 
To conclude my comparison, the graphical user interface and the command-line user interface are both ways to access directories within and navigate a computer. More straightforward for beginners, the GUI is a picture-based setup that allows users to interact with their computer via visuals. The CLI operates just the same, but uses direct text commands and back and forth responses from the computer. In comparison, they both work well with their own unique attributes, pros, and cons. They are simply two interfaces intended for different kinds of users, meant to achieve the same purpose of accessing directories in a computer.
